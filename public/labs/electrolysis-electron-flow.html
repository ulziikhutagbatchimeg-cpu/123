Electrolysis: electron flow in the circuit

Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620, Topic 5: Electrochemistry

Electrolyte
Speed 4
Electron (e⁻): moves through the external circuit only Cation (positive ion): attracted to the cathode Anion (negative ion): attracted to the anode Conventional current (+ to −)
Half-equation at the cathode (negative electrode): reduction
Half-equation at the anode (positive electrode): oxidation
Electrons transferred: 0

Key points

  • Electrolysis is the decomposition of an ionic compound, when molten or in aqueous solution, by the passage of an electric current.
  • In the external circuit, the current is carried by electrons. Electrons move from the negative terminal of the power supply to the cathode, and from the anode back to the positive terminal.
  • In the electrolyte, the current is carried by ions, not electrons. Cations move to the cathode and anions move to the anode.
  • At the cathode, cations gain electrons (reduction). At the anode, anions lose electrons (oxidation). Remember OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain.
  • Ionic half-equations show the reaction at each electrode. In a half-equation, the electrons (e⁻) appear on the left at the cathode and on the right at the anode, and the number of electrons balances the charges.
  • Conventional current flows from the positive terminal to the negative terminal, in the opposite direction to electron flow.
  • Electrodes such as graphite or platinum are inert: they conduct electricity but do not react.
  • For concentrated aqueous sodium chloride, hydrogen is produced at the cathode, not sodium, because sodium is more reactive than hydrogen. Chlorine is produced at the anode because the halide ion is concentrated.
  • For dilute aqueous sodium chloride, hydrogen is produced at the cathode and oxygen at the anode, because the chloride ion is too dilute to be discharged. Hydroxide ions from water are discharged instead.
  • For dilute sulfuric acid, hydrogen is produced at the cathode and oxygen at the anode. The volume of hydrogen is twice the volume of oxygen (2 : 1), because 4 electrons give 2H₂ at the cathode but only 1O₂ at the anode. Sulfate ions are not discharged.
  • Test for hydrogen: a lighted splint gives a squeaky pop. Test for oxygen: it relights a glowing splint. Test for chlorine: it bleaches damp litmus paper.
